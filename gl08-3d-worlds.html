How We Build 3D Worlds

The Quest asks: how does a machine draw a world? The Splat Lab gave one answer: fuzzy blobs. It turns out there is a whole family of answers, and games, films and phones each pick one. Today you meet the whole family, and you get to spin, tap, dig and melt every one of them.

⏱ ~30 min   no lesson needed first   sequel to the Splat Lab

1 · The one trick they all share

Whatever a world is made of, the screen is flat. So every method makes the same first move: put everything at a spot in space, x across, y up, z away. Then a camera looks. Each spot gets flattened onto the glass by dividing by how far away it is. Far things shrink. Grown-ups call that the 1 over z rule.

x on screen = f × x ÷ depth     y on screen = f × y ÷ depth

f is how far the glass is from your eye. Depth is how far the spot is from the camera.

1 · Stuff at (x, y, z) → 2 · Camera looks → 3 · Divide by depth → 4 · Flat picture

You already built this glass in Sketch Lab: Flatten, where a wireframe cube spins with this exact sum. Every toy on this page looks through one shared camera with that one formula. Spin one toy, scroll down, and the others have turned too. Same glass. The only question left is: what stuff do we put in space?

2 · Triangles (the usual way)

Cover the shape in a skin of flat triangles. Three corners in space, one flat patch of colour. A few big triangles make a boxy shape. Hundreds of tiny ones make a skin so smooth your eye stops seeing the corners. A smooth-looking dragon in a film is just thousands of tiny flat triangles.

This is what the Quest is about, and it is what most games and films use, because graphics chips are built to draw triangles very, very fast.

At what number does it start looking round to you? ④ Spin the ball and watch one triangle go round the back. The computer skips every triangle that faces away. Why is that safe?

3 · Cubes (Minecraft's way)

Instead of a smooth skin, fill the world with blocks. Every block is its own little box, so the world is easy to build and easy to change: take a box away and you have dug a hole. Nothing else needs fixing.

Grown-ups call a 3D block a voxel. Some medical scans and a lot of voxel art work the same way. Ours is a heightfield: a grid of columns, each with a height.

🧊 A world of columns

Drag to spin. Tap a column to build it one block taller. Turn on Dig and a tap takes one block away. New hills rolls a fresh seed, like GL 7.

Loading hills…
Things to try: ① Build a tower five high in one corner. Spin round. Which of its sides get drawn, and which get skipped? ② Dig a moat all the way round a hill. ③ Flatten, then build a staircase. How many taps? ④ Look at the result line: the computer only draws the faces it can see. Bury a column between two tall ones and watch the count drop.

4 · Fuzzy blobs (splats), the new way

No skin and no blocks: thousands of soft, see-through blobs floating in space, painted far-to-near. Great for turning a real photo into 3D, because the computer can learn where every blob should sit by comparing with the photo. That whole story is in the Splat Lab.

🫧 A tree made of 120 blobs

Drag to spin. Every time it repaints, all 120 blobs are sorted back to front first, exactly like the Splat Lab.

120 blobs. Far ones painted first, near ones last.
Things to try: ① Spin the tree until an apple goes behind the leaves. Which blobs are painted last then? ② Drag up to look from above. Can you still tell it is a tree? ③ Compare with the triangle ball: where are the crisp edges here? (There are none.) ④ Guess how many blobs a real garden capture uses. Hint: millions.

5 · Shapes from math (no polygons at all)

Here the shape is a formula. There are no triangles, no blocks, no blobs to store. The computer asks every dot on the screen one question: are you inside? If yes, paint it. Because the formula answers for every dot exactly, the edge is perfectly smooth at any zoom.

This trick was invented in the 1980s to draw molecules. The shapes below are called metaballs: each ball pulls on the space around it, and where two pulls add up to enough, the balls melt together. The same question works in 3D to make smooth, melting, blobby shapes.

🌋 Lava lamp

Slide gap to pull the two big balls apart and watch them split. Drag any ball by its centre ring. Every dot is asked "are you inside?" again only when something moves.

Its edge is still perfectly round, because it is a formula, not triangles. ④ Where does the light come from? Move a ball and watch the shine stay on the same side.

📓 Homework (out loud, with Dad)

  1. The Lego build. You want to put your Lego build inside a game. Which builder would you use, and why? Now imagine you want other players to be able to take bricks off. Does your answer change?
  2. Count the triangles. Find a smooth-looking thing in a game or a film. Say out loud what it is really made of. Then find something blocky. Same question.
  3. Challenge: the formula shape asks every dot "are you inside?". Invent a formula for a doughnut. Hint: how far is the dot from a ring?
For grown-ups: read one section aloud, then hand over the iPad and let JJ play the panel until the "things to try" are done. Sections 1 to 3 are one walk, 4 to 6 another. The camera is shared on purpose: spinning any toy spins them all, which is the whole point of section 1.
